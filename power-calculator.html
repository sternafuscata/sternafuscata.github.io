How the homes powered calculation works

Power plants are rated by nameplate capacity, the maximum output in megawatts (MW). No plant runs at full output all year, so the calculator multiplies capacity by the capacity factor to get average output, then divides by the average power a home uses.

Homes powered = MW × capacity factor × 1,000 ÷ avg. home kW

Example: a 1,000 MW offshore wind project with a 40% capacity factor averages 400 MW, or 400,000 kW. Divided by 1.18 kW per home, that is about 339,000 U.S. homes.

Know only the average output? If you have a source’s average power rather than its nameplate capacity, enter that average in megawatts and set the capacity factor to 100%. The result is the number of homes that average output can supply.

Frequently asked questions

How many homes does 1 MW power?

It depends on the source. At 100% output, 1 MW (1,000 kW) would supply about 850 average U.S. homes (1,000 ÷ 1.18 kW). After accounting for capacity factor, 1 MW of solar powers roughly 200 homes, 1 MW of onshore wind about 290 homes, and 1 MW of nuclear about 770 homes.

What is capacity factor?

Capacity factor is a plant's actual electricity output over a year divided by what it would produce running at full nameplate capacity every hour. A 1,000 MW plant with a 40% capacity factor produces the same energy over a year as a 400 MW plant running nonstop.

Where does the 1.18 kW average home demand come from?

The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports that U.S. residential customers used an average of 865 kWh of electricity per month in 2024, or about 10,380 kWh per year. Dividing by the 8,760 hours in a year gives an average continuous demand of about 1.18 kW. Household use is lower in many other countries, so adjust the slider for other regions.

Does “homes powered” mean the plant can supply those homes at every moment?

No. The figure compares a plant's average annual output with average household use. Actual demand peaks on hot afternoons and cold evenings, and wind and solar output varies with the weather, so the grid relies on a mix of sources and storage to match supply and demand hour by hour.
